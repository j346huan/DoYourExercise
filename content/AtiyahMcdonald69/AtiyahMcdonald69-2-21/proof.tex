For $k\geq i,j$, define
\[\alpha_i(x)\alpha_j(y)=\alpha_k(\alpha_{ik}(x)\alpha_{jk}(y)).\]
Any two choices of $k$ have a common upper bound, where the products agree. By \exref{2.15}, equal representatives agree at some later stage, so changing representatives also leaves the product unchanged. The ring axioms follow by moving the finitely many elements involved to one stage. The identity is $\alpha_i(1)$, independent of $i$, and each $\alpha_i$ preserves multiplication and identity.

If $A=0$, choose $i$. Since $\alpha_i(1)=0$, \exref{2.15} gives $j\geq i$ with $\alpha_{ij}(1)=0$. Ring homomorphisms preserve identity, so $1=0$ in $A_j$.
